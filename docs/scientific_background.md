# Scientific background

## Part I — Disease Biology

### DMD: disease profile and natural history

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is a progressive and ultimately fatal X-linked recessive muscle disease affecting approximately 1 in 5,000 males, making it one of the most common recessive disorders in humans. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)

Boys with DMD appear normal at birth. Muscle weakness first becomes apparent in proximal limb muscles before spreading distally. The clinical trajectory follows a predictable and severe course:

- **Ages 2–3**: waddling gait, frequent falls, difficulty rising from the floor (Gowers' sign), and trouble climbing stairs.
- **Ages 10–12**: most patients require a wheelchair as lower limb muscles fail.
- **Adolescence**: progressive scoliosis and joint contractures develop, promoting restrictive lung disease. Assisted ventilation is required between ages 15–20.
- **Ages 20–30**: the majority of patients die from respiratory or cardiac failure even with optimal care.

Death reflects two parallel disease processes. In cardiac muscle, dystrophin absence leads to myocyte death, fibrosis, and secondary fatty infiltration, producing dilated cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and congestive heart failure. In respiratory muscle, diaphragmatic weakness causes severe ventilatory insufficiency, and a diminished cough reflex increases infection risk. A subset of patients also lacks brain dystrophin isoforms, increasing risk of seizures, cognitive impairment, and neurological abnormalities.

---

### The dystrophin gene and mutation landscape

DMD is caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene, the largest gene in the human genome at approximately 2.4 megabases, spanning 79 exons. Its exceptional size accounts for its high spontaneous mutation rate: approximately one-third of cases arise from de novo mutations rather than inheritance. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)

More than 7,000 distinct mutations have been identified across DMD and the allelic disorder Becker Muscular Dystrophy (BMD):

- **~60%** are large deletions
- **~11%** are duplications
- **~10–15%** are nonsense mutations introducing a premature stop codon
- The remainder are small frameshift indels or splice site variants

Mutational hotspots are concentrated in **exons 3–9** and **exons 45–55**, which together account for the majority of large deletions. The wide variety of mutation types, positions, and sizes is the central challenge for developing mutation-specific therapies: each approach works for a defined subset of patients, not all. This heterogeneity is the primary data problem this project addresses.

---

### Dystrophin protein architecture and function

Dystrophin forms a mechanical link between the cytoskeletal actin network inside the muscle cell and the extracellular matrix (ECM) outside. This link protects the sarcolemma from the forces generated by repeated muscle contraction and relaxation. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)

The protein has four main functional domains:

- **N-terminal actin-binding domain (ABD1)**: two calponin homology domains (CH1 and CH2) that anchor dystrophin to cytoskeletal F-actin.
- **Central rod domain**: 24 spectrin-like repeats separated by four hinge regions. Acts as a molecular shock absorber. Contains a second actin-binding domain (ABD2) that works in concert with ABD1 to form a strong lateral actin association. Also mediates interaction with microtubules via repeats 20–23, organising the microtubule lattice in skeletal muscle.
- **Cysteine-rich domain**: anchors dystrophin to the sarcolemma via interaction with transmembrane β-dystroglycan.
- **C-terminal domain**: binds dystrobrevin and syntrophins, mediating sarcolemmal localisation and membrane protein organisation.

In the absence of dystrophin, consequences cascade through multiple pathways:

1. **Membrane instability**: loss of the ECM–sarcolemma–cytoskeleton link creates a leaky sarcolemma highly susceptible to contraction-induced injury.
2. **Calcium dysregulation**: uncontrolled calcium influx through the damaged membrane disrupts calcium homeostasis and muscle contraction signalling.
3. **Sustained inflammation**: damaged cells leak intracellular proteins, triggering continuous immune surveillance, cell infiltration, and chronic inflammation.
4. **Mitochondrial dysfunction**: calcium dysregulation impairs mitochondrial energetics, reducing ATP production and increasing reactive oxygen species — a mechanism thought to particularly affect the diaphragm.
5. **Fibrosis**: the chronic damage–repair cycle generates excessive connective tissue, reducing functional muscle mass and making remaining healthy muscle a smaller target for therapy.

### The reading frame rule

The reading frame rule is the biological principle that connects a patient's specific mutation to their disease severity and their eligibility for mutation-specific therapies. It is the computational core of this project.

The ribosome reads mRNA as a continuous sequence of codons — triplets of nucleotides that each encode one amino acid. The reading frame is established at the start codon and must be maintained throughout the transcript for the ribosome to reach the natural stop codon and produce a complete protein. If the number of nucleotides in a deleted or duplicated region is not divisible by three, the frame shifts at the mutation site. Downstream of the shift, every codon is misread, and the ribosome encounters a premature stop codon within a short distance, terminating translation and producing a truncated, non-functional protein that is rapidly degraded.

**Frame effect computation**: for any deletion or duplication, the reading frame effect is fully deterministic from the sizes of the affected exons. Each exon contributes a fixed number of nucleotides to the mRNA, established by the reference genome:

```
reading_frame_effect = (sum of deleted or duplicated exon sizes, in nucleotides) mod 3
```

- **Result = 0**: in-frame. The reading frame is preserved downstream. The ribosome produces a truncated but partially functional dystrophin → **BMD phenotype** (milder course, ambulatory into adulthood).
- **Result = 1 or 2**: out-of-frame. The reading frame is disrupted downstream. No functional dystrophin is produced → **DMD phenotype** (severe, wheelchair-dependent by adolescence).

The milder symptoms of Becker's disease provide the proof of concept for exon skipping therapy. A patient with an in-frame deletion of, for example, exons 48–51 has BMD and may remain ambulatory into their 30s despite losing a large portion of the central rod domain. This demonstrates that an internally truncated dystrophin retains enough mechanical function to substantially slow disease progression. The therapeutic implication follows directly: if an out-of-frame DMD deletion can be made in-frame by skipping an additional exon during mRNA splicing, the patient's effective phenotype shifts from DMD to BMD-like.

Which exon to skip is dictated entirely by the arithmetic of frame restoration. For a deletion of exon 50 (out-of-frame), skipping exon 51 restores the frame. For a deletion of exons 48–50 (also out-of-frame), skipping exon 51 also restores the frame for different arithmetic reasons. The same AON drug therefore treats multiple distinct deletion patterns, and determining which patients are eligible for which skip requires applying the frame computation to each patient's specific deleted exon list.

This computation was applied systematically to the full Leiden DMD mutation database by [Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19156838/), producing the eligibility tables that approved AON therapies are based on. The exon sizes for the canonical DMD transcript (NM_004006) are fixed and published at [dmd.nl](https://www.dmd.nl). The `reading_frame_effect` field in this project's data model is therefore a computed column derived from these reference sizes — it is never manually annotated.

The rule has well-understood exceptions (~8% of cases): mutations near the 5′ end of the gene and mutations disrupting specific functional domains can produce mismatched phenotypes. These exceptions do not invalidate the rule; they define the boundaries of the deterministic layer and identify where clinical judgment (Layer 3 in the classification framework) is required.

## Part II — Therapeutic Landscape

### Therapeutic approaches

Therapies for DMD fall into two categories: **mutation-agnostic** (benefit all patients regardless of their specific variant) and **mutation-specific** (require knowledge of the patient's exact exon-level mutation to determine eligibility). This distinction defines the structure of the data problem.

#### Mutation-agnostic therapies

**Glucocorticosteroids** (deflazacort, prednisone) are the current standard of care and the most widely used treatment. They delay disease progression through several proposed mechanisms: increasing muscle mass and strength via insulin-like growth factor stimulation, decreasing inflammation, reducing lymphocyte activity, and enhancing myoblast proliferation. The exact mechanism is not fully understood. Glucocorticosteroids address secondary symptoms only and carry significant long-term side effects (weight gain, short stature, puberty delay, behavioural issues, pathological bone fractures). All patients are eligible. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)

**Microdystrophin gene therapy** delivers a shortened but functional version of the dystrophin gene via adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors, providing a functional copy in muscle cells regardless of the underlying mutation. The full dystrophin gene is too large for AAV packaging, so engineered microdystrophin constructs encoding only the critical functional domains are used. Systemic delivery is required to reach all muscles. Current limitations: AAV does not effectively target satellite cells (muscle stem cells), so transduced cells are gradually diluted through normal muscle regeneration; AAV administration can trigger immune responses neutralising the therapy over time; and patients with pre-existing anti-AAV antibodies are excluded. All patients are mutation-eligible; AAV antibody status is a hard patient-level exclusion criterion.

**Non-dystrophin compensatory strategies** target proteins that partially substitute for dystrophin function and therefore work regardless of the underlying mutation:

- *Utrophin modulation*: utrophin is a functional and structural paralog of dystrophin, highly expressed in developing muscle but downregulated post-natally. Small molecules upregulating utrophin in vivo have shown benefit in animal models; utrophin has also been delivered genetically. Because patients have a functional copy of utrophin already, immune response risk is lower than for dystrophin-directed gene therapy.
- *Alpha-7 integrin modulation*: a membrane protein complex with similar anchoring functions to dystrophin. Its upregulation in DMD patients and the more severe phenotype in double-mutant animals suggest it partially compensates for dystrophin loss.
- *Myostatin inhibition*: myostatin is an endogenous inhibitor of muscle growth; blocking it increases muscle mass and has shown some benefit in preclinical studies.

**Membrane stabilisers** (e.g., poloxamer P188) address the primary consequence of dystrophin absence — sarcolemmal leakiness — rather than its cause. They slow calcium influx and reduce the secondary damage cascade. Mutation-agnostic.

**Cell transplantation** aims to deliver cells carrying functional dystrophin into dystrophin-deficient muscle. Requirements include autologous derivation (to minimise immune rejection), ability to cross the vascular wall for systemic delivery, and high muscle engraftment efficiency. No approach has yet met all three requirements reliably. Mutation-agnostic in principle; delivery remains the primary barrier.

#### Mutation-specific: reading frame restoration

These approaches work by correcting the disrupted reading frame in patients with out-of-frame deletions or duplications. Eligibility requires knowing which exons are deleted and whether skipping a specific exon restores the frame.

**AON-mediated exon skipping**: antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) are 20–30 nucleotide modified DNA or RNA molecules that bind their target exon in the pre-mRNA before splicing, masking it from the splicing machinery so it is excluded from the mature mRNA. The result is a shorter but in-frame transcript producing a BMD-like truncated dystrophin. AONs are mutation-specific at the exon level: each approved drug targets one exon and rescues a defined set of deletion patterns. As the underlying genomic mutation persists, repeated administration is required indefinitely.

| Approved AON | Target exon | Eligible deletion patterns | Population coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eteplirsen | Exon 51 | Out-of-frame deletions rescued by skipping exon 51 | ~13–14% |
| Golodirsen | Exon 53 | Out-of-frame deletions rescued by skipping exon 53 | ~8–10% |
| Viltolarsen | Exon 53 | Same as golodirsen | ~8–10% |
| Casimersen | Exon 45 | Out-of-frame deletions rescued by skipping exon 45 | ~8–10% |

Exon 44 skipping could reach an additional ~6% of patients; a drug is in trials. Delivery to myofiber nuclei — where the pre-mRNA target resides before splicing — remains the principal limiting factor for efficacy.

**CRISPR-based reading frame correction**: multiple CRISPR strategies apply the same frame restoration principle as AON exon skipping but make permanent genomic edits rather than requiring repeated dosing. The key approaches and their eligibility profiles are:

| CRISPR strategy | Mechanism | Eligible mutation types | Estimated coverage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exon deletion | Dual sgRNA removes mutant exon(s) via NHEJ | Out-of-frame deletions in hotspot regions | ~60% |
| Exon reframing | Single sgRNA near splice site creates small indels restoring frame | Subset of out-of-frame deletions | — |
| Exon skipping by editing | Single sgRNA disrupts splice site, preventing exon inclusion | Similar to AON targets but permanent | — |
| Base editing | Cytidine or adenine deaminase fused to Cas9 nickase; no double-strand break | Single point mutations (A:T→G:C or C:G→G:C) | ~25–30% |
| Prime editing | Cas9 nickase + reverse transcriptase; pegRNA template | Small frameshift and point mutations within ~50 bp edit range | Subset of point/small mutations |
| Homology Independent targeted integration (HITI) exon knock-in | NHEJ-based insertion in post-mitotic cells | Small deletions requiring exon replacement | ~20% — see caveat |

HITI caveat: NHEJ introduces indels at junction sites, and for a reading-frame-sensitive gene this is a genuine liability. Integration site design must place cuts in intronic regions. All CRISPR approaches are currently in animal model stages; human trials have not yet been initiated for most strategies. Delivery uses the same AAV vectors as microdystrophin gene therapy, carrying the same immunological challenges.

#### Mutation-specific: point mutations and nonsense variants

**Stop codon read-through**: approximately 10–15% of DMD patients carry a nonsense mutation that introduces a premature termination codon (PTC). Read-through agents bind to the ribosome and reduce its ability to recognise the PTC as a stop signal, allowing translation to continue and produce a full-length or near-full-length dystrophin. Eligibility requires a confirmed nonsense mutation; efficacy depends on the specific PTC type (UAA / UAG / UGA) and surrounding nucleotide sequence. Systemic toxicity from promoting generalised codon read-through is the primary safety concern.

---

### Current approvals and trial status

- **Four AONs FDA-approved**: eteplirsen (exon 51, 2016), golodirsen (exon 53, 2019), viltolarsen (exon 53, 2020), casimersen (exon 45, 2021). Collectively reach approximately 27% of DMD patients based on approved indication criteria — a figure established by [Leckie, Zia & Yokota (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/).
- **Microdystrophin gene therapies**: four AAV-delivered constructs in various stages of clinical trials. Early results have been disappointing — limited evidence of functional benefit and significant adverse events related to immune responses.
- **Stop codon read-through**: ataluren showed positive preclinical results; failed primary endpoints in pivotal human trials.
- **CRISPR approaches**: animal studies only at this stage for most strategies.

## Part III — The Gap and Prior Art

### The unmet matching problem

The [Leckie et al. (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/) finding is the quantitative statement of the problem: current FDA-approved therapies reach only 27% of DMD patients. The other 73%  (who may qualify for open trials testing CRISPR deletion, base editing, prime editing, or combination exon skipping) have no accessible, structured mechanism to identify which trials their specific mutation qualifies them for.

The matching logic exists in the literature. The eligibility algorithm was established by Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009). ClinicalTrials.gov holds trial eligibility criteria. LOVD and TREAT-NMD hold curated mutation data. But these sources are siloed: mutation data is in variant databases, trial eligibility is in free text, and the rule engine connecting them exists only in individual research groups. None are connected in a governed, versioned, continuously maintained way.

The result is that patient-trial matching requires manually cross-referencing mutation records, applying the reading frame rule, and parsing free-text eligibility criteria — a process that does not scale across a growing trial landscape.

---

### Prior art and related approaches

Several groups have attempted parts of this problem. Understanding what they built and where they stopped informs what is novel here.

**DMD-specific mutation-to-therapy eligibility matching**

The methodological foundation was established by [Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19156838/) who applied the reading frame rule to the full Leiden DMD mutation database and systematically determined which exon(s) to skip for each deletion pattern. This paper defines the eligibility algorithm this project encodes as a computed field. The most recent application of the same logic is [Leckie, Zia & Yokota (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/), who re-ran every single, double, and multi-exon skipping strategy against the UMD-DMD database and found current FDA-approved AONs reach only 27% of patients, a finding directly relevant to the value of this data product.

The [TREAT-NMD Global Database (Bladen et al. 2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4405042/) aggregated >7,000 mutations from national registries and applied the reading frame classification computationally. It is the largest published DMD mutation dataset and a primary target for data ingestion.

**What these approaches do not do**: none of them provide a systematic, structured, versioned linkage between mutation eligibility and the current landscape of clinical trials. They address a point-in-time eligibility calculation against approved therapies; this project extends that to a live, cross-domain data product updated as trials open and close.

**GA4GH Phenopackets — the patient encoding standard**

[Jacobsen et al. (2022, *Nature Biotechnology*)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-022-01357-4) defines the Phenopacket Schema — a GA4GH standard for encoding a patient's variant, disease, and phenotype in a single computable record. A Phenopacket for a DMD patient encodes the dystrophin variant (HGVS via GA4GH VRS), disease diagnosis (OMIM:310200), and phenotypic features (HPO terms with onset and severity modifiers). It is the most relevant interchange format for the `patient_mutation_profile` data product if cross-institutional sharing of patient profiles is ever required. A practical introduction is available at [Ladewig et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10000265/).

**RDCA-DAP and the Duchenne Regulatory Science Consortium**

[Barrett et al. (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10673974/) describe the Rare Disease Cures Accelerator Data and Analytics Platform (RDCA-DAP), a Critical Path Institute / NORD initiative that aggregates rare disease patient data from clinical trials and natural history studies. The [Duchenne Regulatory Science Consortium (D-RSC)](https://c-path.org/program/duchenne-regulatory-science-consortium/) feeds DMD-specific data into this platform and has published a CDISC Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy Therapeutic Area User Guide — a clinical data standard directly relevant to the `silver.eligibility_criteria` design. RDCA-DAP uses **CDISC SDTM** as its data standard rather than OMOP, reflecting its regulatory submission focus. Trial data encountered from D-RSC sources will be CDISC-encoded.

**OMOP CDM genomic extension**

The OHDSI community has developed a [Genomic CDM (G-CDM) extension](https://github.com/OHDSI/Genomic-CDM) adding four tables: `GENOMIC_TEST`, `TARGET_GENE`, `VARIANT_OCCURRENCE`, and `VARIANT_ANNOTATION`. [Ahmadi et al. (2024, *Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases*)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11325822/) describe how OMOP CDM was adapted for rare diseases, adding Genotype and Phenotype modules and mapping HPO codes via `SOURCE_TO_CONCEPT_MAP`. The limitation is honest: HPO is not a native OMOP vocabulary and the genomic extension remains a prototype. VCF-to-OMOP coverage for rare disease variants has been reported at ~50%.

**Implication for this project**: OMOP is the right standard for the clinical domain (trial eligibility, patient outcomes), but the genomic domain is better served by HGVS + GA4GH VRS for variant representation, with Phenopackets as the patient-level interchange format. The data model proposed here deliberately keeps the two separate rather than forcing genomic data into the OMOP genomic extension prematurely.

**MatchMaker Exchange**

[Philippakis et al. (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4610002/) describe the MatchMaker Exchange (MME), a federated API connecting rare disease patient databases (DECIPHER, PhenomeCentral, GeneMatcher) for cross-institutional patient matching. MME uses HPO + candidate genes as the query format. It is primarily a gene discovery tool for patients without a molecular diagnosis, so it is less directly applicable to DMD where the gene is known. However, the federated matching architecture is a useful reference for how cross-institutional patient queries can be structured without centralising patient data.

### Scientific contribution of this project

The scientific knowledge needed to match DMD patients to therapies already exists. The reading frame eligibility algorithm was established by [Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19156838/). Mutation registries (TREAT-NMD, LOVD) hold curated variant data. ClinicalTrials.gov holds trial eligibility criteria. But these sources are siloed and none of them are connected in a governed, versioned, continuously maintained way.

The contribution of this project is **translational data infrastructure**: taking knowledge that exists in the literature and in scattered databases and making it continuously operational. Specifically, three things that no prior system does simultaneously:

1. **Connecting the mutation layer to the trial layer** — TREAT-NMD knows the mutations; ClinicalTrials.gov knows the trial criteria; this project formally links them with versioning, data contracts, and a defined update cadence.
2. **Extending eligibility logic beyond approved therapies** — all published eligibility analyses focus on the four approved AONs. The trial landscape now includes CRISPR deletion, base editing, prime editing, and gene therapy approaches with more complex eligibility logic. This project encodes that in a queryable, structured system.
3. **Open data product distribution** — making the mutation catalogue and trial eligibility data available via Databricks Marketplace and API for small biotechs and academics who cannot access bespoke analysis.

The sharpest motivation is the [Leckie et al. (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/) finding: current approved therapies reach only 27% of DMD patients. The other 73% may qualify for open trials or experimental approaches — but identifying which ones requires manually cross-referencing mutation data, the reading frame rule, and free-text trial eligibility criteria. That process does not scale. This project makes it queryable.

The contribution is infrastructure, not a new biological finding. In bioinformatics, resource papers — databases, tools, and platforms that others build on — are a legitimate and frequently cited form of scientific output. The [FAIR data principles (Wilkinson et al. 2016)](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618) exist precisely because the gap between scientific knowledge and usable scientific data requires engineering effort that academia rarely rewards but science consistently needs.

## Part IV — Data Architecture

### Classification system for therapeutic suitability

Patient-therapy matching in DMD is a three-layer problem. The layers must be kept separate because they compose differently for each patient — a patient can be mutation-eligible for a therapy but excluded on clinical grounds, or vice versa.

#### Layer 1 — Mutation-intrinsic classification

Every patient record must first be classified on these axes before any therapy logic is applied:

| Axis | Values |
|------|--------|
| Variant class | Large deletion · Duplication · Nonsense · Small frameshift indel · Splice site · Deep intronic |
| Exons affected | List of exon numbers (1–79) |
| Reading frame effect | In-frame / Out-of-frame *(computed — never manually annotated)* |
| Hotspot region | Exons 3–9 · Exons 45–55 · Other |
| Stop codon type | UAA / UAG / UGA *(nonsense mutations only)* |

**Reading frame computation**: the frame effect of any deletion or duplication is fully deterministic from the sizes of the affected exons, which are fixed in the reference genome. The Leiden Muscular Dystrophy pages ([dmd.nl](https://www.dmd.nl)) publish the exon sizes and a reading frame table for all standard deletion patterns.

#### Layer 2 — Approach-specific eligibility rules

Each therapeutic approach applies a logical gate to Layer 1 properties:

| Approach | Mechanism | Primary mutation criterion | Coverage |
|----------|-----------|--------------------------|----------|
| **AON exon 51 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 51 | ~13–14% |
| **AON exon 53 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 53 | ~8–10% |
| **AON exon 45 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 45 | ~8–10% |
| **AON exon 44 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 44 | ~6% |
| **Stop codon readthrough** | Ribosomal suppression | Nonsense mutation (PTC) only | ~10–15% |
| **Microdystrophin gene therapy** | AAV-mediated replacement | Mutation-agnostic | All — Layer 3 dominant |
| **CRISPR exon deletion** | Dual-cut NHEJ removing mutant exons | Out-of-frame deletion in hotspot, amenable to dual-cut excision | ~60% |
| **CRISPR exon reframing** | Single-cut NHEJ restoring frame | Out-of-frame deletion or small indel near splice site | Subset |
| **Base editing** | Nucleotide conversion without DSB | Single point mutation (A·T→G·C or C·G→G·C only) | ~25–30% |
| **Prime editing** | Reverse transcriptase nick correction | Small frameshift or point mutation within edit range (~50 bp) | Subset |
| **HITI exon knock-in** | NHEJ-based insertion in post-mitotic cells | Small deletion requiring exon replacement | ~20% — see caveat |
| **Utrophin / non-dystrophin** | Compensatory upregulation | Mutation-agnostic | All |
| **Glucocorticosteroids** | Anti-inflammatory / standard of care | Mutation-agnostic | All |

**HITI caveat**: NHEJ introduces indels at junction sites. For a reading-frame-sensitive gene like dystrophin, this is a genuine liability. Integration site must be designed into an intronic region to prevent junction indels from disrupting the ORF. Eligibility assessment for HITI should flag this risk explicitly. **REVIEW THIS If you knock an exon or part of an exon in, it will by definition be in the exon region?**

#### Layer 3 — Patient-level criteria

Applied after mutation eligibility is confirmed. These are trial- and therapy-specific and will evolve as approvals change:

- **Age and ambulatory status**: most approved and trial therapies have age windows and require ambulatory function at baseline
- **Pre-existing AAV antibodies**: hard exclusion for all AAV-based gene therapies; titres against the specific serotype must be below threshold
- **Cardiac and respiratory function**: baseline FVC and ejection fraction thresholds are common inclusion criteria
- **Prior treatment**: some trials exclude patients who have received other gene therapies or specific AONs

### Data model

The model follows the **Databricks medallion architecture** and is organised across two data mesh domains: **Discovery** (genetic and variant data) and **Clinical** (trial eligibility and patient-therapy matching). The Gold layer of each domain produces data products consumed cross-domain to generate the final matching output.

#### Domain map

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┐     ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
│        DISCOVERY DOMAIN         │     │          CLINICAL DOMAIN          │
│  (genetic & variant data)       │     │  (trial eligibility & matching)   │
│                                 │     │                                   │
│  Bronze  Silver  Gold           │     │  Bronze    Silver    Gold         │
│  ──────  ──────  ────           │     │  ──────    ──────    ────         │

│  HGMD       records             │     │                      eligibility  │
│                                 │     │                                   │
│  Ensembl ─► exon ─► patient_ ──────►  (consumed by Clinical gold layer) │
│  dmd.nl      ref    mutation_   │     │                                   │
│              data   profile     │     │                                   │
└─────────────────────────────────┘     └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Discovery domain — layer by layer

**Bronze** *(raw ingestion, no transformation)*

| Table | Source | Contents |
|-------|--------|----------|
| `bronze.lovd_variants_raw` | LOVD API / export | Raw variant records as received |
| `bronze.clinvar_submissions_raw` | ClinVar FTP | Raw XML/VCF submissions |
| `bronze.ensembl_exons_raw` | Ensembl REST API | DMD transcript, exon coordinates, sizes |

**Silver** *(cleaned, normalised, enriched)*

| Table | Derived from | Key transformations |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| `silver.dmd_variants` | LOVD + ClinVar | HGVS normalisation; exon mapping; deduplication across sources |
| `silver.exon_reference` | Ensembl + dmd.nl | Exon number, genomic coordinates, size (bp), reading frame contribution (0/1/2) |
| `silver.variant_classification` | ClinVar | Pathogenicity tier per variant; evidence level |

**Gold — data products** *(published interfaces consumed by other domains)*

| Data product | Contents | Key computed fields |
|-------------|----------|-------------------|
| `gold.dmd_mutation_catalogue` | All known DMD/BMD variants, normalised and classified | Variant class, exons affected, pathogenicity |
| `gold.patient_mutation_profile` | Per-patient mutation summary | `reading_frame_effect` *(computed from exon sizes)*; `variant_class`; `hotspot_region`; `stop_codon_type` |
| `gold.exon_skipping_eligibility` | Per-patient, per-approved-skip eligibility | Boolean eligibility flag per AON (exon 51/53/45/44); reasoning trace |

The `reading_frame_effect` field in `patient_mutation_profile` is the critical computed field. It derives from joining the patient's deleted/duplicated exon list against `silver.exon_reference` and summing the frame contributions modulo 3. This is deterministic and should never be manually annotated.

#### Clinical domain — layer by layer

**Bronze** *(raw ingestion)*

| Table | Source | Contents |
|-------|--------|----------|
| `bronze.clinicaltrials_raw` | ClinicalTrials.gov API | Full JSON records per NCT ID |
| `bronze.eu_trials_raw` | EU Clinical Trials Register | European trial records |
| `bronze.fda_approvals_raw` | FDA drug labels | Approved therapies and prescribing criteria |

**Silver** *(structured eligibility rules)*

| Table | Derived from | Key transformations |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| `silver.trials_dmd` | ClinicalTrials + EU register | Filtered to DMD trials; phase; status; intervention type |
| `silver.eligibility_criteria` | `trials_dmd` + FDA approvals | Structured inclusion/exclusion rules — split into `mutation_criteria` and `patient_criteria` rows |

**Gold — data products**

| Data product | Contents | Cross-domain dependency | Use case |
|-------------|----------|------------------------|----------|
| `gold.trial_eligibility_catalogue` | Per-trial structured eligibility rules, versioned | None — self-contained | Patient-trial matching; cohort sizing |
| `gold.patient_trial_eligibility` | Per-patient, per-trial eligibility verdict with mutation-eligible flag, evidence level, and exclusion reasons | Consumes `discovery.gold.patient_mutation_profile` | Patient-trial matching; patient-therapy matching |
| `gold.therapy_addressable_population` | Per therapy or trial: count and breakdown of patients meeting mutation eligibility | Consumes `discovery.gold.dmd_mutation_catalogue` | Therapeutic cohort sizing |
| `gold.mutation_coverage_gaps` | Per mutation class: covered by ≥1 approved therapy / covered by trial only / uncovered entirely | Consumes `discovery.gold.dmd_mutation_catalogue` + `gold.trial_eligibility_catalogue` | Mutation gap analysis |
| `gold.patient_trial_eligibility_delta` | Row-level delta between consecutive versions — new matches, lost eligibility due to trial status changes or criteria revisions | Consumes `gold.patient_trial_eligibility` (versioned) | Proactive trial alerts |

#### Cross-domain link

`patient_trial_eligibility` is the primary cross-domain data product. It is owned by the Clinical domain but reads the Discovery domain's published `patient_mutation_profile`. The join logic applies Layer 2 eligibility rules (mutation criteria) and Layer 3 criteria (patient-level) from `trial_eligibility_catalogue` against the mutation classification from `patient_mutation_profile`.

```
discovery.gold.patient_mutation_profile
        +
clinical.gold.trial_eligibility_catalogue
        │
        ▼  (eligibility rule engine)
clinical.gold.patient_trial_eligibility
  ├── patient_id
  ├── nct_id
  ├── mutation_eligible      (bool)
  ├── patient_eligible       (bool — requires clinical record input)
  ├── evidence_level         (approved / trial / experimental)
  └── exclusion_reasons[]
```

`discovery.gold.patient_mutation_profile` is a published data product with a defined contract. The Clinical domain subscribes to it as a consumer. Changes to the Discovery domain's output schema require versioning and notification to downstream consumers per the data contract for `patient_mutation_profile`.
