# Scientific background

## Duchenne disease: a rare genetic disease with complex genetic architecture
###  Pathophysiological basis of DMD Phenotype: Disease profile: symptoms, incidence etc
- Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is a progressive and ultimately fatal muscle disease. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- It causes muscle wasting, leading to skeletal muscle wasting, respiratory insufficiency and cardiomyopathy. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- DMD is an X-linked recessive disorder. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- It affects 1 in 5000 boys and is one of the most common recessive disorders in humans. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- Boys with DMD appear normal at birth, but muscle weakness and wasting becomes first apparent in proximal limb muscles before spreading to distal limbs.[Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- Diagnosis usually occurs with the first few years of life. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
    - Waddling gait, frequent falls, difficulties standing up from a sitting position, (Gower’s sign), and struggling to climb stairs are observed between ages 2 and 3.
    - By ages 10-12, most of the patients require weelchairs.
    - With progressive muscle weakness comes scoliosis and joint contractures develop, which promote restrictive lung disease.
    - Assisted ventilation is necessary from the ages 15-20 years old.
    - Majority of DMD patients die between ages 20-30 years old from respiratory or cardiac failure, even with optimal care.
        - Dystrophin absence in cardiac muscles results in cardiac myocyte death, which in turn results in fibrosis and secondary fatty acid infiltration, which itself results into decreased systolic/diastolic function (blood pressure) and atrophy and remodelling in the ventricular myocardium.
        - This leads to dilated cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, and congestive heart failure        .
        - Weakened respiratory muscles in the diaphragm cause severe ventilatory insufficiency.
        - Diminished coughing response increases the risk of infections.

    - Additionally, a lack of brain dystrophin increases the risk of seizures, lowered IQ and neurological abnormalities.


### Genetic architecture: The mutations causing Duchenne disease
- DMD is caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- Dystrophin function:
    - Dystrophin forms a mechanical link between cytoskeletal actin and the extracellular matrix (ECM), which protects the sarcolemma from the force associated with stretch and contraction. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
    - Dystrophin organizes membrane proteins and its absence leads to impairments in cell signalling and progressive muscle waisting. [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- The dystrophin gene is very large (How large?) causing a high mutation rate.[Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- More than 7000 mutations have been identified for DMD or the Becker Muscular Dystrophy (BMD).[Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.
    - 60% are deletions
    - 11% are duplications
    - The remainder are small mutations affecting the coding sequences or splice sites.
- Mutational hotspots are in exons 3-9 and 45-55 [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- The high mutation rate and different ways in which frameshift can occur are challenging for developing gene corrective therapies for patients with different mutations. 


### Protein architecture and function
[Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- Dystrophin has 4 main functional protein domains [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
    - an N-terminal region with homologies to the actin-binding domains of α-actinin that anchors the protein to the cytoskeleton.
        - The N-terminal domain includes two calponin homology domains (CH1 and CH2). 
        - This conventional CH-actin binding domain binds directly to F actin, anchoring dystrophin to the cytoskeleton
    -  a central rod domain containing 24 spectrin-like repeats separated by four hinge regions
        - The spectrin-like repeats in the central rod of dystrophin indicate that dystrophin works as a shock absorber, helping to resist repeated rounds of muscle contraction and relaxation. 
        - The rod domain also harbors a second actin-binding motif (ABD2). ABD2 is found near the middle of the rod and works in concert with ABD1 to form a strong lateral association with actin filaments. 
        - The rod domain also mediates dystrophin interaction with microtubules via spectrin-like repeats 20–23 and is required for the organization of the microtubule lattice in skeletal muscle cells.
    - a cysteine-rich domain that anchors dystrophin to the muscle membrane via interaction with the transmembrane protein β-dystroglycan;
    - a C-terminal domain that binds dystrobrevin and syntrophins, mediating sarcolemma localization.
- The absence of dystrophin leads to:
    - membrane instability due to the lack of a physical link between the sarcolemma, ECM and cytoskeleton. This causes a leaky sarcolemma that is highly susceptible to injury.
    - Leaky sarcolemmas cause calcium specifically to leak into muscle cells, disrupting calcium homeostasis Calcium is needed to contract muscles.
    - The immune system plays a key role in clearing damaged muscle fibers. It uses the release of proteins from damaged cells as an indicator of damage. 
      The leaky DMD muscle cells constantly release these proteins, resulting in a sustained immune response, immune cell infiltration and inflammation
    - The problems with calcium homeostasis also impair mitochondrial energetics and decreasing ATP production.
    - Electrons leaked from impaired electron transport chains in the mitochondria also lead to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) in DMD muscle cells.This mechanism seems to particularly impact muscle diaphragm weakness.
    - Fibrosis is a wound-healing process wherein an excessive amount of connective tissue is formed, leading to permanent scar tissue. In DMD, fibrosis decreases contractile function and reduces the amount of healthy muscle for therapy. 

### The reading frame rule and Beckers disease
- The difference between BMD and DMD is that the mutations in BMD lead to loss of some exon functionality, but still proper expression of the dystrophin gene, whereas the dystrophin gene is completely non-functional in DMD.


### Therapeutic strategies as a data problem [Angulski et al. (2023)](papers/Angulski2023.pdf)
- There is no current cure for DMD. A multidisciplinary medical, surgical, and rehabilitative approach targeting the symptoms of DMD can alter the natural course of the disease, improving longevity and quality of life.
- Small molecule therapeutics:
    - Glucocorticosteroids, such as deflazacort or prednisone, are the current standard of care and the most widely used treatment for DMD patients.
    - The exact mechanism by which glucocorticosteroids delay disease progression in DMD is not fully understood. However, it has been suggested that glucocorticosteroids increase total muscle mass and strength in patients with DMD through the stimulation of insulin-like growth factors, decreased inflammation, decreased lymphocyte reaction and cytokine production, and enhanced myoblast proliferation.
    - Glucocorticosteroids only partially treat the secondary symptoms of DMD and long term use comes  with side effects (weight gain, short stature, puberty delay, behavioral issues, and pathologic bone fractures)
- Microdystrophin viral gene therapy:
    - Viral-mediated gene therapy has long been considered a potential therapeutic strategy to treat DMD based on its capability to restore the missing dystrophin by providing a functional copy of the dystrophin gene or repairing dystrophin, thereby restoring dystrophin throughout the body.
    - Replacement gene therapy fixes the problem irrespective of the underlying mutation, making it attractive for DMD in particular.
    - Replacement gene therapy has been challenging for two reasons:
        - The large size of the dystrophin gene causes problems for viral vector packaging
        - The treatment needs to reach all muscles in the body, requiring a whole body-systemic therapy.
        - The most common viral vectors implemented for neuromuscular disorders are lentiviruses and adeno-associated viruses (AAV). Systemic delivery has been achieved for the microdystrophin gene, but the method does not permanently integrate a functional copy leading to dilution and loss of effectiveness.
        - As AAV vectors do not effectively target satellite cells, upon muscle injury, the satellite cells that are recruited to take part in the repair process will not contain the therapeutic transgene, causing the transduced cells to become diluted and eventually disappear with time.
        - AAV administration can induce immune responses and antibody production, rendering the treatment ineffective over time.
        - Some patients have pre-existing anti-AAV antibodies, which excludes them from this therapy immediately.
        - Research into how to modulate this immune response is ongoing as well as optimizing viral delivery and expression cassettes.
        - Another active area of research is how much of the dystrophin gene you need to create a functional protein. This has lead to the development of microdystrophins.

- Exon skipping:
    - The goal of exon skipping is to restore a correct open reading frame, allowing the creation of a functional protein and hence turning the severe DMD phenotype in the comparatively milder Becker phenotype.
    - It works by manipulating mRNA splicing to bypass defective exons and producing a partially functional dystrophin gene.
    - Exon skipping typically results in larger proteins than the microdystrophins that are currently under investigation.
    - Exon skipping can be achieved using CRISPR-Cas DNA manipulation, U7 snp RNA mediated inhibition of splicing or antisense oligonucleotide drugs that modulate RNA splicing.
    - AON mediated exon skipping:
        - AONs are small 20–30 nucleotides of modified DNA or RNA homologs that specifically bind to their target exon prior to premRNAsplicing i n a Watson-Crick-like manner,hiding the target exon from the splicing machinery so that it is spliced out with its flanking introns blocking its incorporation to the mature mRNA. 
        - Antisense-mediated exon skipping is a method that targets a specific mutation.
        - Hence, it is crucial to have a precise genetic diagnosis of the disease to be able to design effective AONs.
        - Certain exons have been reported to have the highest applicability for skipping. Namely, exon 51 could be applied for (13%–14%) of patients,  exon 45 and exon 53 both could be skipped for another 8%–10% of patients, and exon44 could be skipped for an additional 6% of the patients 
        - A major challenge for the success of AON approaches centers on achieving adequate delivery to the myofiber nuclei so that the drug can hit its pre-mRNA target in the nucleus (prior to splicing) and prevent the splicing of the targeted exon.  People use cell transfections, in vivo intramuscular injections, and in vivo systemic delivery (intravenous, intraperitoneal, or subcutaneous).
        - The (unstated) advantage is that you know need to deliver much smaller molecules in a systemic way, opening up nanoparticles etc. Nevertheless, as the underlying mutations that cause DMD disease remain and due to transcript and protein turnover, repeated drug injection is required. 
        - Despite the attractiveness and applicability of single or multiple exon(s) skipping techniques and the fact that four AONs have already been approved for clinical trials, it is becoming clear that their delivery efficiency is not satisfactory. (Bad for the patients, but if this approach works it is a strong support for a data mesh because you will need to assess mutation suitability for many different cocktails.)
- Stop codon read-through:
    - Around 10%–15% of patients with DMD have a nonsense mutation that induces a premature termination codon (PTC) in the mRNA, causing the ribosome to terminate translation and failing to synthesize the remainder of the protein. (If you have a mutation like this, it should also be in the mesh)
    - The principle of stop codon read-through is to induce the ribosome to continue translating the mRNA through the premature stop codon and continue through the rest of the transcript. The drug works by binding to the ribosome and its partners, disrupting its ability to recognize the premature stop codon, leading to the transformation of the PTC into a correct codon, thereby resulting in the continuation of the translation.
    - A number of drugs can induce stop codon read-through, and their efficacies rely on the nature of the nonsense mutation and the surrounding nucleotide sequences.
    - The upside of this is that you can use known small molecules to treat DMD, but the downside is the toxic effects of promoting codon read-through in general.


- CRISPR-Cas9 mediated gene editing: All of these approaches require you to know precisely which gene you need to target.
    - Gene editing: Correct the gene at the genome level
        - Even partially succesful interventions (deletions of mutant exons etc) can restore the ORF and improve the condition.
        - Permanent repair of even a small number of cells can enable generation of dystrophin in skeletal fibers since they are multinucleated.
        - Dystrophin is an X-linked gene and a male disease, meaning that unintentionally altering a wild-type copy is not possible.
        - This treatment is in the animal studies stage, but not humans.
    - Exon deletion: Cut out mutant exons or hotspot regions to restore the ORF
        - Program two sgRNA's to double-cut the mutant region and remove.
        - Disadvantage: shorter dystrophin genes than other methods
        - Can be used to treat 60% of patients
        - Deletion efficiency seems to decrease with longer fragments.
    - Exon skipping by gene editing
        - Program one sgRNA to target the splice site and delete the exon splicing sequence via non homologous end joining (NHEJ)
        - Increases editing efficiency compared to two sgRNA's
    - Exon reframing by gene editing:
        - Program one sgRNA to make a cut and restore the reading frame via targeted small insertion-deletions
        - This approach preserves a larger portion of the dystrophin gene.
    - Exon knock-in:
        - Program a single sgRNA to make a cut into the dystrophin gene and use homology directed repair and a DNA piece with the correct exon to restore the mutant exon
        - Has the potential to recover the full dystrophin gene.
        - HDR is not active in quiescent or G1 arrested cells, so it doesn't work in mature myofibers and cardiac myocytes.
        - Homology Independent targeted integration (HITI) does work in post-mitotic cells.
        - Needs additional delivery of a donor plasmid with two Cas9 cleavage sites surrounding the donor plasmid. NHEJ still works here, but is imprecise in the junction sites, which is a liability for the ORF.
        - Great promise, but efficiency is still low and applies to 20% of DMD patients (why? Because their mutations are small enough?)
    - Base editing
        - Roughly 25-30% of DMD patients have point mutations.
        - Base editing is about converting A:T to G:C pairs and reverse
        - Base editing works by coupling a Cas9 nickase to a cytidine deaminase and an adenine deaminase.
        - The method only works for single point mutations, but is not error prone like HITI and doesn't require a donor sequence.
        - It can also be used to induce exon skipping.
    - Prime editing:
        - Also used to treat point mutations.
        - Uses a PE2 protein which consists of a Cas9 nickase fused to a reverse transcriptase enzyme and a pegRNA (prime editing guide RNA)
        - The pegRNA guides to complex to the correct genomic location and carries the desired edit sequence as an RNA template.
        - A nick is introduced, exposing a 3'end which hybridizes with a sequence on the pegRNA called the primer binding site (PBS)
        - The reverse transcriptase then uses the pegRNA as a template for create a new strand in DNA which creates a flap.
        - Conventional cell machinery can then resolve and incorporate the corrected flap.
        - It handles small corrections without introducing DNA breaks or needing HDR to be active.
        - The size of the PE2 protein and pegRNA (which is also larger then usual) create delivery problems.

    - Cas9 delivery:
        - Viral delivery: AAV vectors remain the most popular method
        - Non viral delivery: Can be delivered in RNA, DNA form, via electroporation, lipid mediated nanoparticles, gold nanoparticles
    - Future challenges:
        - off target effects: Drive Cas9 expression using muscle specific promoters
        - Systemic delivery
        - Maintenance of long term dystrophin expression: Cardiac myocytes are long-lived and stable but skeletal muscle cells regenerate all the time.
        - Immunological responses against Cas9 or the new dystrophin


- Cell transplantation therapy: Transplant cells with functional dystrophin into dystrophin deficient muscles
    - Requirements: 
        - Autogenic: manipulation of the patients own cells rather than donor cells decreases the risk of immune response
        - Able to pass the vascular wall to enable systemic delivery without needing multiple injections
        - High muscle engraftment rate to provide long-term effect and offset the possibility of an immune response to repeated delivery.
    - Several cell types under investigation, but delivery remains a huge issue with this approach.

- Small molecule therapy:
    - The primary effect of dystrophin is stabilizing the membrane and preventing leakage.
    - Small molecule membrane stabilizers can also help with slowing down muscle damage.
    - Poloxamers like P188 can help with that and offset the negative effects of calcium influx

- Non dystrophin approaches:
    - Some genetic strategies target not dystrophin itself, but alternatives that compensate for lack of dystrophin function
    - The advantage of these approaches is that they help regardless of the mutation.
    - Utrophin modulation:
        - A functional and structural paralog of dystrophin.
        - Highly expressed in developing muscle
        - Increased utrophin expression helps mitigate the effect of dystrophin absence
        - Small molecules are used to upregulate utrophin production in vivo
        - Additionally, utrophin has also been delivered and knocked in genetically like dystrophin. The advantage of utrophin delivery is that the patient has a healthy version of it already, reducing the risk of an immune response.
        - However, utrophin and dystrophin are not fully the same.
    - alpha7-Integrin modulation:
        - A protein complex that also has similar anchoring and structuring functions as dystrophin.
        - Upregulation in DMD patients and more severe symptoms of double mutants suggests it compensates for dystrophin loss.
        - Laminin-111 is another potential candidate of interest 
    - Myostatin, follistatin and other muscle growth strategies: Myostatin is an inhibitor of muscle growth and in some studies has been shown to increase muscle strength.


- Overview of clinical trials and approved strategies:
    - 4 AAV delivered microdystrophins are in various stages of clinical trials, but results have been disappointing showing lack of positive effects and a lot of negative side effects.
    - Alturan is an anti stop codon small molecule drug with positive results in mouse studies, but not humans.
    - AON's have better results, but still lack efficacy it seems.


## Classification system for therapeutic suitability TO BE REVIEWED AS I GO ALONG AI SUGGESTION

Patient-therapy matching in DMD is a three-layer problem. The layers must be kept separate because they compose differently for each patient — a patient can be mutation-eligible for a therapy but excluded on clinical grounds, or vice versa.

### Layer 1 — Mutation-intrinsic classification

Every patient record must first be classified on these axes before any therapy logic is applied:

| Axis | Values |
|------|--------|
| Variant class | Large deletion · Duplication · Nonsense · Small frameshift indel · Splice site · Deep intronic |
| Exons affected | List of exon numbers (1–79) |
| Reading frame effect | In-frame / Out-of-frame *(computed — see note below)* |
| Hotspot region | Exons 3–9 · Exons 45–55 · Other |
| Stop codon type | UAA / UAG / UGA *(nonsense mutations only)* |

**Reading frame computation**: the frame effect of any deletion or duplication is fully deterministic from the sizes of the affected exons, which are fixed in the reference genome. This should be a computed field in the data model, not a manually annotated one. The Leiden Muscular Dystrophy pages ([dmd.nl](https://www.dmd.nl)) publish the exon sizes and a reading frame table for all standard deletion patterns.

### Layer 2 — Approach-specific eligibility rules

Each therapeutic approach applies a logical gate to Layer 1 properties:

| Approach | Mechanism | Primary mutation criterion | Coverage |
|----------|-----------|--------------------------|----------|
| **AON exon 51 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 51 | ~13–14% of patients |
| **AON exon 53 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 53 | ~8–10% of patients |
| **AON exon 45 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 45 | ~8–10% of patients |
| **AON exon 44 skip** | mRNA splicing modulation | Out-of-frame deletion rescued by skipping exon 44 | ~6% of patients |
| **Stop codon readthrough** | Ribosomal suppression | Nonsense mutation (PTC) only | ~10–15% of patients |
| **Microdystrophin gene therapy** | AAV-mediated replacement | Mutation-agnostic | All — Layer 3 dominant |
| **CRISPR exon deletion** | Dual-cut NHEJ removing mutant exons | Out-of-frame deletion in hotspot, amenable to dual-cut excision | ~60% of patients |
| **CRISPR exon reframing** | Single-cut NHEJ restoring frame | Out-of-frame deletion or small indel near splice site | Subset of deletions |
| **Base editing** | Nucleotide conversion without DSB | Single point mutation (A·T→G·C or C·G→G·C only) | ~25–30% of patients |
| **Prime editing** | Reverse transcriptase nick correction | Small frameshift or point mutation within edit range (~50 bp) | Subset of point mutations |
| **HITI exon knock-in** | NHEJ-based insertion in post-mitotic cells | Small deletion requiring exon replacement | ~20% of patients — see caveat |
| **Utrophin / non-dystrophin** | Compensatory upregulation | Mutation-agnostic | All |
| **Glucocorticosteroids** | Anti-inflammatory / standard of care | Mutation-agnostic | All |

**HITI caveat**: NHEJ introduces indels at junction sites. For a reading-frame-sensitive gene like dystrophin, this is a genuine liability. Integration site must be designed into an intronic region to prevent junction indels from disrupting the ORF. Eligibility assessment for HITI should flag this risk explicitly.

### Layer 3 — Patient-level criteria

Applied after mutation eligibility is confirmed. These are trial- and therapy-specific and will evolve as approvals change:

- **Age and ambulatory status**: most approved and trial therapies have age windows and require ambulatory function at baseline
- **Pre-existing AAV antibodies**: hard exclusion for all AAV-based gene therapies; titres against the specific serotype must be below threshold
- **Cardiac and respiratory function**: baseline FVC and ejection fraction thresholds are common inclusion criteria
- **Prior treatment**: some trials exclude patients who have received other gene therapies or specific AONs

## The data model integrating data sources for Duchenne disease

The model below follows the **Databricks medallion architecture** and is organized across two data mesh domains: **Discovery** (genetic and variant data) and **Clinical** (trial eligibility and patient-therapy matching). The gold layer of each domain produces data products that are consumed cross-domain to generate the final matching output.

### Domain map

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┐     ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
│        DISCOVERY DOMAIN         │     │          CLINICAL DOMAIN          │
│  (genetic & variant data)       │     │  (trial eligibility & matching)   │
│                                 │     │                                   │
│  Bronze  Silver  Gold           │     │  Bronze    Silver    Gold         │
│  ──────  ──────  ────           │     │  ──────    ──────    ────         │
│  LOVD ─► norm ─► mutation   ───────►──►  CTgov ─► eligib ─► patient_   │
│  ClinVar    variant   catalogue │     │  FDA/EMA   rules     trial_      │
│  HGMD       records             │     │                      eligibility  │
│                                 │     │                                   │
│  Ensembl ─► exon ─► patient_ ──────►  (consumed by Clinical gold layer) │
│  dmd.nl      ref    mutation_   │     │                                   │
│              data   profile     │     │                                   │
└─────────────────────────────────┘     └──────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Discovery domain — layer by layer

**Bronze** *(raw ingestion, no transformation)*

| Table | Source | Contents |
|-------|--------|----------|
| `bronze.lovd_variants_raw` | LOVD API / export | Raw variant records as received |
| `bronze.clinvar_submissions_raw` | ClinVar FTP | Raw XML/VCF submissions |
| `bronze.ensembl_exons_raw` | Ensembl REST API | DMD transcript, exon coordinates, sizes |

**Silver** *(cleaned, normalized, enriched)*

| Table | Derived from | Key transformations |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| `silver.dmd_variants` | LOVD + ClinVar | HGVS normalization; exon mapping; deduplication across sources |
| `silver.exon_reference` | Ensembl + dmd.nl | Exon number, genomic coordinates, size (bp), reading frame contribution (0/1/2) |
| `silver.variant_classification` | ClinVar | Pathogenicity tier per variant; evidence level |

**Gold — data products** *(published interfaces consumed by other domains)*

| Data product | Contents | Key computed fields |
|-------------|----------|-------------------|
| `gold.dmd_mutation_catalogue` | All known DMD/BMD variants, normalized and classified | Variant class, exons affected, pathogenicity |
| `gold.patient_mutation_profile` | Per-patient mutation summary | `reading_frame_effect` *(computed from exon sizes)*; `variant_class`; `hotspot_region`; `stop_codon_type` |
| `gold.exon_skipping_eligibility` | Per-patient, per-approved-skip eligibility | Boolean eligibility flag per AON (exon 51/53/45/44); reasoning trace |

The `reading_frame_effect` field in `patient_mutation_profile` is the critical computed field. It derives from joining the patient's deleted/duplicated exon list against `silver.exon_reference` and summing the frame contributions modulo 3. This is deterministic and should never be manually annotated.

### Cross-domain link

`patient_trial_eligibility` is the primary cross-domain data product. It is owned by the Clinical domain but reads the Discovery domain's published `patient_mutation_profile` data product. The join logic applies the Layer 2 eligibility rules (mutation criteria) and Layer 3 criteria (patient-level) from `trial_eligibility_catalogue` against the mutation classification from `patient_mutation_profile`.

```
discovery.gold.patient_mutation_profile
        +
clinical.gold.trial_eligibility_catalogue
        │
```

## Prior art and related approaches

Several groups have attempted parts of this problem. Understanding what they built and where they stopped informs what is novel here.

### DMD-specific mutation-to-therapy eligibility matching

The methodological foundation was established by [Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19156838/) who applied the reading frame rule to the full Leiden DMD mutation database and systematically determined which exon(s) to skip for each deletion pattern. This paper defines the eligibility algorithm this project encodes as a computed field. The most recent application of the same logic is [Leckie, Zia & Yokota (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/), who re-ran every single, double, and multi-exon skipping strategy against the UMD-DMD database and found current FDA-approved ASOs reach only 27% of patients — a finding directly relevant to the value of this data product.

The [TREAT-NMD Global Database (Bladen et al. 2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4405042/) aggregated >7,000 mutations from national registries and applied the reading frame classification computationally. It is the largest published DMD mutation dataset and a primary target for data ingestion.

**What these approaches do not do**: none of them provide a systematic, structured, versioned linkage between mutation eligibility and the current landscape of clinical trials. They address a point-in-time eligibility calculation against approved therapies; this project extends that to a live, cross-domain data product updated as trials open and close.

### GA4GH Phenopackets — the patient encoding standard

[Jacobsen et al. (2022, *Nature Biotechnology*)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41587-022-01357-4) defines the Phenopacket Schema — a GA4GH standard for encoding a patient's variant, disease, and phenotype in a single computable record. A Phenopacket for a DMD patient encodes the dystrophin variant (HGVS via GA4GH VRS), disease diagnosis (OMIM:310200), and phenotypic features (HPO terms with onset and severity modifiers). It is the most relevant interchange format for the `patient_mutation_profile` data product — if cross-institutional sharing of patient profiles is ever required, Phenopackets is the standard to target. A practical introduction is available at [Ladewig et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10000265/).

### RDCA-DAP and the Duchenne Regulatory Science Consortium

[Barrett et al. (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10673974/) describe the Rare Disease Cures Accelerator Data and Analytics Platform (RDCA-DAP), a Critical Path Institute / NORD initiative that aggregates rare disease patient data from clinical trials and natural history studies. The [Duchenne Regulatory Science Consortium (D-RSC)](https://c-path.org/program/duchenne-regulatory-science-consortium/) feeds DMD-specific data into this platform and has published a CDISC Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy Therapeutic Area User Guide — a clinical data standard directly relevant to the `silver.eligibility_criteria` design. RDCA-DAP uses **CDISC SDTM** as its data standard rather than OMOP, reflecting its regulatory submission focus. This is worth noting because trial data encountered from D-RSC sources will be CDISC-encoded.

### OMOP CDM genomic extension

The OHDSI community has developed a [Genomic CDM (G-CDM) extension](https://github.com/OHDSI/Genomic-CDM) adding four tables: `GENOMIC_TEST`, `TARGET_GENE`, `VARIANT_OCCURRENCE`, and `VARIANT_ANNOTATION`. [Ahmadi et al. (2024, *Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases*)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11325822/) describe how OMOP CDM was adapted for rare diseases, adding Genotype and Phenotype modules and mapping HPO codes via `SOURCE_TO_CONCEPT_MAP`. The limitation is honest: HPO is not a native OMOP vocabulary and the genomic extension remains a prototype. VCF-to-OMOP coverage for rare disease variants has been reported at ~50%.

**Implication for this project**: OMOP is the right standard for the clinical domain (trial eligibility, patient outcomes), but the genomic domain is better served by HGVS + GA4GH VRS for variant representation, with Phenopackets as the patient-level interchange format. The data model proposed here deliberately keeps the two separate rather than forcing genomic data into the OMOP genomic extension prematurely.

### MatchMaker Exchange

[Philippakis et al. (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4610002/) describe the MatchMaker Exchange (MME) — a federated API connecting rare disease patient databases (DECIPHER, PhenomeCentral, GeneMatcher) for cross-institutional patient matching. MME uses HPO + candidate genes as the query format. It is primarily a gene discovery tool for patients without a molecular diagnosis, so it is less directly applicable to DMD where the gene is known. However, the federated matching architecture is a useful reference for how cross-institutional patient queries can be structured without centralising patient data.

## Scientific contribution of this project AI GENERATED SUBJECT FOR REVIEW

The scientific knowledge needed to match DMD patients to therapies already exists. The reading frame eligibility algorithm was established by [Aartsma-Rus et al. (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19156838/). Mutation registries (TREAT-NMD, LOVD) hold curated variant data. ClinicalTrials.gov holds trial eligibility criteria. But these sources are siloed and none of them are connected in a governed, versioned, continuously maintained way.

The contribution of this project is **translational data infrastructure**: taking knowledge that exists in the literature and in scattered databases and making it continuously operational. Specifically, three things that no prior system does simultaneously:

1. **Connecting the mutation layer to the trial layer** — TREAT-NMD knows the mutations; ClinicalTrials.gov knows the trial criteria; this project formally links them with versioning, data contracts, and a defined update cadence.
2. **Extending eligibility logic beyond approved therapies** — all published eligibility analyses focus on the four approved AONs. The trial landscape now includes CRISPR deletion, base editing, prime editing, and gene therapy approaches with more complex eligibility logic. This project encodes that in a queryable, structured system.
3. **Open data product distribution** — making the mutation catalogue and trial eligibility data available via Databricks Marketplace and API for small biotechs and academics who cannot access bespoke analysis.

The sharpest motivation is the [Leckie et al. (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11593839/) finding: current approved therapies reach only 27% of DMD patients. The other 73% may qualify for open trials or experimental approaches — but identifying which ones requires manually cross-referencing mutation data, the reading frame rule, and free-text trial eligibility criteria. That process does not scale. This project makes it queryable.

The contribution is infrastructure, not a new biological finding. In bioinformatics, resource papers — databases, tools, and platforms that others build on — are a legitimate and frequently cited form of scientific output. The [FAIR data principles (Wilkinson et al. 2016)](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618) exist precisely because the gap between scientific knowledge and usable scientific data requires engineering effort that academia rarely rewards but science consistently needs.

The roadmap prioritises the **Clinical domain first** — ingesting and structuring trial eligibility data before building the mutation matching layer. This delivers immediate analytical value (which trials exist for DMD?) and provides an early, concrete encounter with the clinical data standards (CDISC, OMOP, ICD, HPO) that will govern the rest of the data model.

**Goal**: produce `gold.trial_eligibility_catalogue` as the first published data product.

**What you learn in this phase**: ClinicalTrials.gov eligibility criteria are largely free text.
