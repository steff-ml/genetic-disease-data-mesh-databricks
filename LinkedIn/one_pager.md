# The bottleneck is not the science: how data silos stifle medical progress and real-world care

## One pager: Implementing a data mesh for rare genetic diseases on Databricks

---

A child with Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) receives a confirmed genetic diagnosis on average 2.2 years after first symptoms appear [Thomas et al., 2022]. By that point, a critical piece of their clinical trajectory is — in principle — predictable from a single formula: sum the nucleotide lengths of the deleted exons and divide by three. A remainder of zero means the reading frame is preserved, producing a truncated but partially functional dystrophin protein — the Becker (BMD) phenotype, with symptom onset often deferred to adolescence, the ability to remain ambulatory into the 40s or beyond [Bushby & Gardner-Medwin, 1993], and near-normal life expectancy. A remainder of one or two means the reading frame is disrupted, no functional dystrophin is produced, and the outcome is DMD: loss of independent walking by age 10–12, ventilator support through the teens, and a life expectancy of 20–30 years [Angulski et al., 2023]. The rule holds for 91% of mutations when assessed at the DNA level; when confirmed at the RNA level — where alternative splicing events that restore the reading frame post-transcriptionally are accounted for — this rises to over 99.5% [Aartsma-Rus et al., 2006]. DNA profiling is sufficient for the vast majority of patients and for all exon-skipping eligibility decisions; the 9% of DNA-level exceptions fall into identifiable categories: in-frame deletions removing the functionally indispensable cysteine-rich domain, very large in-frame deletions exceeding 35 exons in the central rod domain, and out-of-frame mutations that inadvertently disrupt exonic splicing enhancer sequences and trigger natural exon skipping. For most patients, this arithmetic determines which treatment a patient can receive. The arithmetic is not the bottleneck. The data infrastructure is.

---

Data silos are both ubiquitous and structural in science and pharma. In scientific discovery, data fragmentation is considered a major obstacle to the effective use of AI — not primarily a technical problem but a social one, rooted in misaligned incentives, institutional boundaries, and the undervaluation of data infrastructure work in hiring and tenure decisions [Channing & Ghosh, 2025]. In rare disease specifically, the consequences are concrete: fragmented knowledge encourages redundant data collection in uncoordinated parallel studies, imposes growing costs on patients and caregivers asked to participate repeatedly across disconnected efforts, and leaves individual organisations unable to accumulate the critical mass of knowledge needed to de-risk drug development [Denton et al., 2021]. These problems compound when handling sensitive data, where silos emerge for organisational, technical, regulatory, and contractual reasons simultaneously.

Addressing this requires domain, regulatory, and architectural expertise to coexist in the same team. Novartis offers one model: embedding AI specialists directly with biologists and chemists — described as building "super-intelligent" teams — and pairing this with enterprise-wide digital literacy programmes so that data capability and scientific expertise reinforce each other rather than operating in separate functions [Intuition Labs, 2024].

---

The harm caused by data silos in rare disease is not abstract. Only four FDA-approved exon-skipping therapies exist for DMD (targeting exons 51, 53, 45, and 44). Together they cover approximately **27% of DMD patients** by mutation alone [Leckie, Zia & Yokota, 2024]. The remaining 73% have options — investigational antisense oligonucleotides, CRISPR-based exon deletion approaches, stop-codon readthrough for the ~13% with nonsense mutations [Aartsma-Rus et al., 2009], and mutation-agnostic microdystrophin gene therapy — but identifying which approach a specific patient qualifies for requires manually cross-referencing their confirmed mutation against the reading frame rule, current trial eligibility criteria in free-text clinical databases, and approved therapy labels across systems that do not interoperate. That process does not scale across a patient population and does not update automatically when new trials open.

In precision oncology, the MatchMiner platform addressed the equivalent problem by computationally linking tumour genomic profiles to targeted trials, reducing the time between tumour genotyping and trial consent by 22% (55 days) [Klein et al., 2022]. The DMD problem is structurally more tractable: unlike tumour heterogeneity, the biology of exon-deletion eligibility follows a rule that holds for 91% of documented mutations [Aartsma-Rus et al., 2006]. The formula is known. The exon sizes are published. What is missing is not scientific inference but governed infrastructure that applies that arithmetic at scale across fragmented databases that hold the mutation records. Curiously, no equivalent platform exists for rare monogenic diseases despite the fact that the matching logic is computationally straightforward once the data is integrated.

---

This project is an open-source reference implementation of a governed data mesh on Databricks that enables patient-trial and patient-therapy matching for DMD, with an architecture designed to extend to SMA, Huntington's disease, and the lysosomal storage disorders — any rare genetic disease where mutation type determines therapy eligibility and patient populations are too small for reactive recruitment to function reliably. FDA-initiated platforms such as RDCA-DAP [Barrett et al., 2023] pursue similar goals using proprietary clinical trial submissions and CDISC SDTM as the regulatory submission format. This project is its open-access counterpart: built on public registries (LOVD, ClinVar, ClinicalTrials.gov), using OMOP CDM for analytical interoperability, and designed for pre-competitive research and hypothesis generation — the upstream layer that identifies gaps and eligibility logic before data enters a regulated submission pipeline.

Databricks is well-suited to this problem for specific reasons. The mutation data exists across multiple registries (LOVD, ClinVar), trial data arrives in unstructured and semi-structured form from multiple sources (ClinicalTrials.gov, EudraCT/CTIS, FDA DailyMed), and the data must be joined, governed, and made queryable while meeting GDPR and ICH E6 GCP requirements simultaneously. A medallion architecture with Unity Catalog governance addresses the variable schemas, update frequencies, and access requirements these sources bring. Bitol ODCS data contracts on Gold tables make the outputs auditable and versioned — a requirement for any output that feeds a clinical decision. Delta Live Tables with declarative quality expectations ensure that variant records with unresolved classification conflicts never reach a downstream eligibility determination without being flagged.

This project is designed to show the power of first-class data governance in driving real-world outcomes and supporting AI innovation in contexts where the cost of a data quality failure is not a wrong dashboard number but a missed treatment window.
